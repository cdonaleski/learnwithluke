How it works
Find it — on your own
The page names a feature and you tap it. A wrong tap tells you what you did touch; a second one shows you where the right one is. Practice only — nothing is recorded.
Name it — with a parent
The week's features light up. Point to each one and name it, in order, the way the proof is given — the whole box, not one at a time. Whoever is checking has the list and marks the week, which is what fills in its dot on the Year at a Glance.
Shapes and points
Where a public map has the real thing — a river's course, a lake, a trail's route, the San Andreas Fault — it is drawn as it is. Where it does not, the feature is a dot at its recorded location. Nothing is sketched in by hand. Two exceptions, said plainly: the Native American regions have no official borders, so Eastern Woodlands and Northwest Coast are marked where Classical Conversations' own map labels them; and Hudson Bay, which is in Canada, is the arrow off the top of the map.
Where the map comes from
Natural Earth and the U.S. Census Bureau (public domain); the National Park Service's Santa Fe Trail; the Bureau of Land Management's national historic trails; the U.S. Geological Survey's fault database; and Wikidata for the locations of single points. None of it is traced from the map in the Foundations Guide.
