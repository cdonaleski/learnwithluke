How it works
Find it — on your own
The page names a feature and you tap it. A wrong tap tells you what you did touch; a second one shows you where the right one is. Practice only — nothing is recorded.
Study — on your own
One feature lights up in yellow. Say what it is out loud, then press Show answer to check yourself, and mark whether you knew it. On Easy it is zoomed in close; on Medium it sits among everything else; on Hard it is alone on a bare map. Practice, so it does not count toward learned — the ones you did not know are listed at the end to go round again.
Recite — with a parent or a friend
The week's features light up. Point to each one and name it, in order, the way the proof is given — the whole box, not one at a time. Whoever is checking has the list and marks the week, which is what fills in its dot on the Year at a Glance. On Hard the map stays bare, so you have to point to them from memory; the checker can press Show where they are to check you.
Easy, Medium, Hard
Easy draws every feature and zooms in close to the one you are asked about, with its nearest neighbors around it to choose between. Medium draws every feature from every week, none of them named, on the whole country — like the Black Line Master: you have to know which triangles are the Cascades. Hard draws nothing at all, so it asks only whether you know where it is: a tap close to the right one counts, even if something else is a little nearer.
The pictures
Drawn the way Classical Conversations' own key draws them: mountain ranges as clusters of little triangles, peaks in red, deserts in orange, water in blue, trails dotted, the Native American regions in pale colors.
Shapes and points
Where a public map has the real thing — a river's course, a lake, a trail's route, the San Andreas Fault — it is drawn as it is. Where it does not, the feature is a dot at its recorded location. Nothing is sketched in by hand. Two exceptions, said plainly: the Native American regions have no official borders, so Eastern Woodlands and Northwest Coast are marked where Classical Conversations' own map labels them; and Hudson Bay, which is in Canada, is the arrow off the top of the map.
Where the map comes from
Natural Earth and the U.S. Census Bureau (public domain); the National Park Service's Santa Fe Trail; the Bureau of Land Management's national historic trails; the U.S. Geological Survey's fault database; and Wikidata for the locations of single points. None of it is traced from the map in the Foundations Guide.
